## 1. Definition and scope

**Spirit** is used across philosophical, religious, and everyday contexts to refer to spiritual vitality, orientation, inspiration, relation to the sacred, or transcendence. In some traditions, it refers to a divine or nonphysical reality. These meanings overlap in some contexts but are not interchangeable. This report does not define spirit as an undiscovered physical energy, measurable field, or substitute for a known biological process.

For this framework, the following are **working distinctions**, not universal definitions for all cultures or religions:

- **Soul:** questions of identity, life, character, and meaning.
- **Spirit:** questions of vitality, inspiration, connection, sacred orientation, and transcendence.
- **Consciousness:** experienced awareness, with the intended meaning specified in context.

These definitions organize this project; they do not claim that all traditions draw the same boundaries. Where a tradition uses *spirit*, *soul*, *breath*, *life*, or related terms differently, the tradition-specific usage takes priority over the project's convenience.

## 2. Evidence and research findings

### 2.1 Textual and doctrinal evidence

**1 Thessalonians 5:23** names spirit, soul, and body. This is evidence that the passage uses a three-term expression; whether it teaches three separable components of a person is a further exegetical question. Commentaries differ: some read the terms as a tripartite anthropology, while others understand the verse as an exhaustive expression for the whole person. A comparative report should record both readings instead of treating the verse alone as proof of three measurable entities. [S31, S32]

**Qur'an 17:85** refers to *al-rūḥ*, variously translated in English as “spirit” or “soul.” The verse says that knowledge concerning the *rūḥ* is limited and refers the matter to God's command. The wording and traditional commentary should be considered before assigning a single modern English equivalent. Classical exegetical traditions discuss the referent and context in different ways; interpretations should not be flattened into the claim that the verse unambiguously means either the human soul or the angel of revelation. [S33, S34]

These passages document religious language and interpretive traditions. They do not, by themselves, establish a cross-tradition scientific taxonomy of spirit, soul, and body.

### 2.2 Mysticism and reported experience

Mysticism is better understood as a constellation of practices, texts, institutions, discourses, and experiences aimed at transformation than as a single type of unusual episode. Reports may describe communion with God, awe, unity with nature, altered self–other boundaries, nondual awareness, or a sense of insight. These descriptions are evidence about what people report and how traditions interpret it; the truth of any claimed external or transcendent object requires additional argument. Philosophers continue to debate how experiences are shaped by language, culture, practice, and prior beliefs, and whether some experiences provide evidence for their interpreted contents. [S30]

A careful analysis separates:

1. **Phenomenology:** what the person reports experiencing.
2. **Interpretation:** the meaning assigned to the experience within a personal, cultural, or religious framework.
3. **Ontology:** what is claimed to exist independently of the experience and its interpretation.
4. **Behavioral or ethical consequences:** how the experience affects a person's conduct, relationships, and commitments.

These levels can influence one another, but none should automatically stand in for another.

### 2.3 The 2026 systematic review of religious and spiritual practices

Husain and colleagues published “The Neuroscience of Religious and Spiritual Practices: A Systematic Review of Neurotheological Evidence” in *Brain and Behavior* in 2026. The review reports screening and synthesizing 105 studies from 1998–2026, covering practices such as meditation, prayer, chanting, worship, retreat, and mystical experience across several traditions. It describes reported involvement of multiple systems associated with attention, emotion regulation, self-referential processing, and reward. [S27]

**Interpretation must remain bounded.** A systematic review of heterogeneous studies can identify patterns and candidate mechanisms, but it does not make every included study equally strong, prove that all spiritual practices have the same effect, or establish the metaphysical truth of a religious interpretation. The article's broad claims about consistent effects and neuroplasticity should be read in light of differences in study design, sample size, measurement, comparison groups, and the distinction between cross-sectional associations and causal evidence. The publication record also lists a subsequent erratum; readers should consult the current version and correction before relying on detailed claims. [S27, S28]

The defensible conclusion for this framework is that **some spiritual and religious practices can be studied empirically through reported experience, behavior, physiology, and neural measures**. The literature does not establish a measurable spiritual substance or decide whether an experience's religious interpretation is true.

### 2.4 Harvard seminar on self-transcendence, April 2026

A Harvard University Department of Psychology calendar entry lists Michael Ferguson's 9 April 2026 seminar, “From Fundamentalism to Mysticism: A Brain Network for Self-Transcendence and Its Subdomains.” The abstract describes lesion network mapping evidence involving patients whose focal brain injuries were associated with lasting changes in self-transcendence, and discusses a proposed circuit relevant to mystical experience. [S29]

This is a seminar abstract, not a complete peer-reviewed paper or a full methods report. Lesion network mapping can contribute evidence about brain networks associated with a phenomenon, but it does not mean researchers have identified a single “spirituality center.” A lesion-based association also does not establish that a neural circuit exhaustively explains spiritual meaning or validates a metaphysical claim. The source supports treating self-transcendence as a researchable construct, while detailed claims await review of the underlying publication, methods, sample, and limitations.

### 2.5 Spiritual vitality versus biological vitalism

“Vitality” may be used in an ordinary or interpretive sense to describe energy for living, courage, devotion, inspiration, purpose, or a felt connection. That use does not require positing a new physical force. Biological explanations of cellular function, metabolism, nervous-system activity, or homeostasis should be evaluated using their own scientific evidence. Spirit language should not be used to replace those mechanisms or to imply that a spiritual practice reliably treats a medical or mental-health condition.

## 3. Perspectives

The report keeps three broad perspectives in conversation without declaring them equivalent:

- **Tradition-internal perspective:** a religious or spiritual community interprets spirit through its own scriptures, practices, authorities, and lived commitments.
- **Phenomenological and psychological perspective:** researchers document experiences, their context, their reported meaning, and their relationship to behavior or well-being.
- **Neuroscientific perspective:** researchers investigate neural and physiological correlates or possible mechanisms associated with particular practices or experiences.

Each perspective answers different questions. A neural correlate does not by itself settle theological truth; a sincere spiritual interpretation does not by itself establish a causal neural theory. A naturalistic account of the conditions of an experience need not settle all questions of its personal significance, while felt profundity alone does not establish the ontology of what was experienced.

## 4. Relationships to the other reports

- **Body:** spiritual practices are performed by embodied people and can be studied through behavior, physiology, and neural measures. The meaning of a practice is not reducible to a physiological measurement.
- **Mind:** concepts, attention, memory, language, expectation, and culture can shape how an experience is noticed and interpreted. How much these factors constitute the experience itself is a continuing scholarly question.
- **Soul:** some traditions distinguish spirit and soul; others overlap them or use the terms contextually. The framework's distinction is a working convention, not a universal translation rule.
- **Consciousness:** spiritual experience is a kind or interpretation of experience, but not every conscious episode is spiritual. “Self-transcendence,” “mystical experience,” and “consciousness” require separate operational definitions in empirical work.
- **Earth/TERRA:** nature-oriented spirituality may motivate a sense of kinship or responsibility toward the living world. Whether that sense entails particular duties of care is an ethical argument; it does not follow automatically from geophysics.
- **Good and Love:** spiritual commitments can motivate compassion and service, but intentions and feelings alone do not guarantee good outcomes. Conduct should also be evaluated by consent, dignity, honesty, consequences, and care.
- **Emotion and Transformative Intelligence:** awe, devotion, fear, gratitude, and inspiration may shape judgment and motivation. Their importance should be respected without treating emotional intensity as proof of a factual or metaphysical claim.

## 5. Uncertainty and limitations

1. There is no single culture-independent definition of spirit accepted across religions, philosophy, and science.
2. Mystical and spiritual experiences vary; scholars disagree about the roles of culture, concepts, practice, and supposedly nonconceptual experience.
3. Neural activity associated with a practice is not proof that a metaphysical interpretation is true or false.
4. A brain network associated with self-transcendence is not a unique “spirituality center,” nor does it establish that all spiritual experiences share one mechanism.
5. Findings from heterogeneous studies should not be generalized to all people, practices, traditions, or clinical outcomes without adequate evidence.
6. The 2026 systematic review has a listed erratum; its current corrected record should be checked before quoting specific numerical or methodological details.
7. The Harvard source located is a seminar abstract, not a complete paper.
8. This report does not validate paranormal capacities, a measurable spiritual field, guaranteed benefits, or any claim that a subjective experience proves an external entity's existence.

## 7. Conclusions

Spirit remains a meaningful category for examining vitality, inspiration, transcendence, sacred orientation, and connection. Current research supports empirical investigation of particular practices and reported experiences, including their neural and behavioral correlates. It does not settle the metaphysical questions that different traditions associate with spirit.

The framework can use spirit as a distinct organizing concept provided it marks that distinction as interpretive, respects tradition-specific meanings, and does not turn neural findings into either proof or disproof of spiritual ontology. The next task is a context-sensitive glossary and primary-text comparison, with each proposed mind–soul–spirit relationship assigned an explicit interpretation and evidence status.

## Research Pass 1 — Neurotheology Update (2025–2026)

### What the systematic review supports

Husain and colleagues' 2026 systematic review synthesized 105 neuroimaging and neurophysiological studies published from 1998 through 2026, spanning eight religious/spiritual traditions and seven principal imaging or neurophysiological modalities. Because the studies were heterogeneous, the authors used a narrative synthesis without meta-analysis. The review reports practice-specific neural patterns and evidence consistent with neuroplastic changes among some long-term practitioners. It provides a broad empirical anchor for the proposition that spiritual practices and reported experiences have measurable neural correlates. [S43]

The claim must remain carefully scoped: a neural signature or measured change supports a claim about nervous-system activity under specified practices and conditions. It does not establish that the content of an experience refers to an independently existing spiritual entity, and it does not settle whether a theological interpretation is true or false. Practice, tradition, participant characteristics, methods, sample sizes, and study quality differ; the synthesis is not equivalent to a single controlled experiment demonstrating one universal “spirituality circuit.”

### Updated claim status

- **EMPIRICAL / SUPPORTED_BUT_SCOPED:** selected spiritual practices are associated with measurable neural and physiological patterns; some studies report practice-related structural or functional change.
- **SPIRITUAL/THEOLOGICAL:** interpretations of sacred reality remain tradition-specific or personal claims, to be represented with attribution.
- **NOT ESTABLISHED BY THESE DATA:** a particular metaphysical ontology or universal neural mechanism of spirituality.

### 1. The 105-study synthesis

Husain et al. (2026) report a systematic review of 105 neuroimaging and neurophysiological studies published from 1998–2026, spanning multiple religious/spiritual traditions, practices, and measurement modalities. The review reports neural and physiological correlates and practice-specific patterns across systems implicated in attention, affect, memory, social cognition, and related functions. The authors used narrative synthesis because of heterogeneity; this is not a single pooled effect estimate and does not justify one-to-one mapping between a brain region and a spiritual tradition or experience. **Correction tracking is mandatory.** The journal published a correction noting that Figure 2 contained visually misleading anatomical representations and labels and should be removed, along with specified text. The correction states that the figure was illustrative and was not used in study selection, data extraction, analysis, or interpretation. Accordingly, the correction matters for accurate citation and presentation of the paper, while the notice does not say the review's overall results were invalidated. ### 2. What the evidence supports—and does not

The evidence supports the modest claim that religious and spiritual practices can be studied as biologically active practices and that measured neural/physiological patterns vary with task, practice, participant, and context. It does **not** establish:
- a dedicated “God spot”;
- that a neural correlate proves or disproves a theological proposition;
- that all traditions' experiences are reducible to one neural signature;
- that measured changes establish the external truth of a spiritual interpretation.

The claim that there is no dedicated religious module should be expressed cautiously: the review documents distributed and practice-sensitive findings, but a broad review of diverse practices is not by itself a decisive anatomical proof that no specialized component could ever be identified. The appropriate conclusion is that current findings do not warrant a simple one-region/one-spirituality model.

### 3. Source record and reproducibility

Record the search window, databases, eligibility rules, heterogeneity, and correction status. Where exact counts or practice-specific findings are repeated, consult the article and its supplementary material rather than relying only on secondary summaries. Distinguish primary-study findings from the review authors' synthesis and from this framework's interpretation.

## 0.7.0 findings
Husain et al.'s 2026 systematic review reports a narrative synthesis of 105 studies spanning 1998–2026, eight religious traditions, and multiple practices and neuroimaging/neurophysiological modalities. Reported patterns include prefrontal and anterior cingulate engagement, DMN-related changes, insula/amygdala/hippocampal and temporoparietal involvement, and differences by practice type. The authors describe meditation, prayer, chanting/recitation, and mystical-state profiles as distinguishable in the included literature.

These are review-level summaries across heterogeneous studies, not universal signatures that occur in every practitioner or every task. Do not write as if “meditation suppresses the DMN,” “prayer activates social cognition,” or “chanting causes limbic deactivation” were exceptionless laws. Results depend on the specific practice, comparison condition, participant experience, measurement modality, analysis choices, and study quality. A narrative synthesis is not a pooled effect-size estimate.

## Correction notice
The journal issued a correction because Figure 2 contained visually misleading anatomical representations and labels; the figure and specified text passages were to be removed. The correction states that the figure was not used in study selection, extraction, analysis, or interpretation, and that the reported results and conclusions were unaffected. This correction must remain attached to any use of the review. Avoid reproducing or relying on the corrected anatomical schematic.

## Interpretation boundary
Neuroplastic or neural correlates can support a mechanistic account of attention, emotion regulation, self-referential processing, reward, and altered experience. They do not establish or disprove the theological truth of a tradition, the existence of God, or the metaphysical status of spirit. “No dedicated spiritual module demonstrated” is more defensible than “science has proved spirituality is only ordinary brain activity.”
