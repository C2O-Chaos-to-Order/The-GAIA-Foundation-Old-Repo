## Scope
“Spirit” may refer to devotion, meaning, moral aspiration, communal identity, transcendent interpretation, or metaphysical reality, depending on the tradition. Neuroscience can investigate practices, reports, physiological changes, and neural correlates; neural activation alone cannot establish the truth or falsity of a theological claim.

## Current evidence synthesis
Husain et al. report a systematic review of 105 empirical studies spanning 1998–2026, multiple religious traditions, and seven neuroimaging/neurophysiological modalities. The review describes practice-associated patterns including DMN and prefrontal changes during meditation, social-cognition network engagement during prayer, and limbic/oscillatory findings during chanting or recitation. It also reports structural neuroplasticity in long-term practitioners. Because studies were heterogeneous, the authors used narrative synthesis rather than quantitative meta-analysis. https://doi.org/10.1002/brb3.71733

These are group-level, task- and method-dependent patterns, not one-to-one neural signatures for “spirit.” Practice type, expectation, culture, attention, emotion, breathing, movement, and social context may all matter. The findings support the claim that religious and spiritual practices can be neurologically active and sometimes associated with plasticity; they do not establish that every practice produces the same effects or that a metaphysical interpretation is verified.

## Correction note
A September 2026 correction states that Figure 2 in the review contained misleading anatomical representations and should be removed, along with specified passages. The authors state that the figure was illustrative and was not used in study selection, extraction, analysis, or interpretation, and that its removal does not change the reported results or conclusions. Cite the correction alongside the review when discussing it in detail: https://doi.org/10.1002/brb3.71785

## Proposed test
Define practice and comparison condition precisely; distinguish immediate state effects from long-term training; preregister neural, behavioural, autonomic, and self-report outcomes; control for breathing, movement, expectancy, familiarity, and social context; report null results and between-person variability. A minimal plasticity model can test consequences of its own assumptions but cannot independently validate real-world neural signatures.

## Interpretation boundaries
- Neural correlates are not proof of theological claims.
- Neural mechanisms do not make lived religious meaning “mere” or insignificant.
- A distributed neural profile does not imply a dedicated spiritual module.
- A plasticity model is not a model of God, spirit, or ultimate reality.
