## 1. Definition and scope

**Consciousness** is used here primarily to mean phenomenal consciousness: subjective experience, or the fact that there is something it is like to see, feel, think, imagine, or undergo a state. In research and philosophy, however, the word is used for several related but non-identical questions. This report distinguishes:

- **Phenomenal consciousness:** the experiential character of a state.
- **Access consciousness:** information being available for reasoning, flexible control, and report.
- **Self-awareness / metacognition:** capacities to represent, monitor, or evaluate oneself or one's own mental states.
- **Wakefulness or level of consciousness:** the broad state of arousal and responsiveness.
- **Contents of consciousness:** what is experienced within a state or level.

These distinctions are analytically useful, but their exact boundaries are disputed. Wakefulness is not the same as a particular experience; attention is not automatically identical to consciousness; and a person's inability to report an experience is not, by itself, proof that no experience occurred. Reports are important evidence, but their absence must be interpreted alongside behavioral, physiological, and contextual evidence. [S01–S10]

The report treats **consciousness of an organism**, **conscious states or contents**, and **consciousness as a proposed fundamental feature of reality** as different claims requiring different evidence. A philosophical or spiritual use of “consciousness” should not silently be substituted for a neuroscientific operational definition.

## 2. Evidence and research findings

### 2.1 What empirical neuroscience can establish

Neuroscience investigates systematic relationships between reported experience, behavior, brain activity, and bodily state. Converging methods can constrain explanations of when conscious contents occur and which neural processes are associated with them. But identifying a neural correlate is not automatically identifying a complete cause, a sufficient mechanism, or an explanation of why physical processes have a subjective aspect.

First-person reports, behavioral measures, neural measurements, and computational models each have limitations. Reports depend on memory, language, task demands, and willingness or ability to respond. Neural signals require choices about measurement, preprocessing, statistical analysis, and interpretation. A useful theory should make predictions that distinguish it from alternatives and specify in advance what outcomes would count against it. [S01–S10]

### 2.2 Competing theories are research programs, not settled facts

**Global Neuronal Workspace Theory (GNWT)** proposes, broadly, that some information becomes conscious when it is made widely available to multiple systems, often described in terms of global broadcasting or ignition.

**Integrated Information Theory (IIT)** attempts to characterize consciousness in terms of a system's intrinsic causal structure and integrated information. Its formal claims and the mapping from the formalism to biological evidence are contested.

**Higher-Order Theories (HOT)** broadly propose that a mental state is conscious in virtue of an appropriate higher-order representation or awareness of that state. Different versions vary substantially in what the higher-order representation must be.

Other approaches, including recurrent-processing and predictive-processing accounts, also contribute to the field. These families are not interchangeable, and their predictions can overlap. None should be presented as an established, complete explanation of phenomenal consciousness. [S01–S10]

### 2.3 The 2025 Cogitate adversarial collaboration

The Cogitate Consortium's preregistered study, “Adversarial testing of global neuronal workspace and integrated information theories of consciousness,” was published in *Nature* on 30 April 2025. It tested human participants (**n = 256**) across six laboratories using functional MRI, magnetoencephalography, and intracranial EEG. The consortium preregistered competing predictions and criteria for interpreting outcomes. The authors reported that the results aligned with some predictions while substantially challenging key tenets of both GNWT and IIT. For example, the reported absence of sustained synchronization in posterior cortex challenged a distinctive IIT prediction; GNWT was challenged by the general lack of the predicted ignition pattern at stimulus offset and by limitations in prefrontal representation of some conscious dimensions. [S11]

**Interpretation:** This was an important empirical comparison, not a final referendum on every version of either theory. The tested predictions were selected operationalizations of theoretical claims, and the article itself distinguishes more theory-specific predictions from predictions shared with other approaches. A theory can be challenged at a prediction without every version of it being refuted; equally, an auxiliary assumption should not be used after the fact to make a failed prediction immune to evidence. The correct assessment requires the preregistration, supplementary methods, prediction weighting, data, and each theory's stated commitments to be read together. The study is more informative than a simple “both theories passed” or “both theories were disproved” summary. [S11–S12]

### 2.4 The 2026 critique of Cogitate: a position to evaluate, not a settled verdict

Arthur Stewart's “Conceded in Advance: How Cogitate Disabled Its Own Verdict” is discoverable as a May 2026 Zenodo preprint (DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.20031607). It argues that the collaboration was structurally “soft-adversarial,” because some tested biological implementations could be separated from the theories' central claims and because predictions overlapped with other theories. It contends that a theory should not be protected by retreating to a core claim that the experiment did not test after its specified predictions fail. [S13]

This is a **critical argument by an individual author**, not a consensus finding or, on the evidence verified for this revision, a peer-reviewed adjudication. Its concerns are methodologically relevant: a test's force depends on how faithfully the operational predictions represent a theory's commitments, how predictions were weighted, and whether post-hoc reinterpretation is constrained. But the critique itself must also be evaluated against the published preregistration, supplementary information, and the authors' explicit account of what was tested. The fact that a criticism exists does not establish that all of its claims are correct.

The supplied title “The experiment that should have broken the field” (reported as a June 2026 Zenodo commentary, S32) could not be independently verified to a reliable bibliographic record during this revision. Its described claims—including that Cogitate was not aimed at the hard problem and that biogenic magnetite, pineal calcite piezoelectricity, and microtubule quantum effects remain unexplored—are therefore **not treated as verified findings or relied upon as evidence**. Even if a paper makes these proposals, the existence of a proposed physical mechanism does not show that it explains consciousness. Each candidate requires a clear mechanism, measurable predictions, evidence of relevance to conscious experience, and comparison with alternatives.

### 2.5 AI systems and consciousness-theory operationalizations

The source titled “Can We Test Consciousness Theories on AI? Ablations, Markers, and Robustness” is identifiable as Yin Jun Phua's arXiv preprint **arXiv:2512.19155, submitted 22 December 2025**, not an August 2026 preprint. Its abstract describes three experiments using artificial agents to test functional consequences of architectural ablations. It reports, among other results, that removing a workspace collapses access-related markers in the tested agent and that self-model ablation affects metacognitive calibration. Crucially, the author explicitly states that the agents are **not conscious** and are reference implementations for testing functional predictions. [S14]

The source metadata and abstract verified for this revision do **not** substantiate the supplied description that architectural variance dominates stimulus variance in six of seven operationalizations. That claim should not be attributed to this paper unless another exact source is supplied and checked.

AI models can be useful for testing whether a proposed architecture produces predicted functional behaviors under controlled interventions. Such results can establish claims about the tested architecture and operational measures. They do not, by themselves, establish phenomenal experience, human-equivalent experience, or a universal consciousness detector. A system can implement memory, self-description, global information routing, or an integration metric without those features alone resolving whether anything is experienced.

### 2.6 The 2026 data-driven connectivity study

Bryant and Whyte's “A data-driven approach to identifying and evaluating connectivity-based neural correlates of conscious visual perception” appeared in *Neuroscience of Consciousness* in 2026 (DOI: 10.1093/nc/niag029; published 7 July 2026). It reanalyzed the Cogitate magnetoencephalography dataset and preregistered hypotheses, comparing 246 functional-connectivity measures. The authors report that a family of barycenter-based measures performed well for stimulus decoding and that their simple GNWT-based and IIT-based neural-mass models both captured aspects of observed connectivity, with somewhat more support for the GNWT-based model in this dataset. They explicitly describe the conclusion as tentative and dataset-specific. [S15]

A correction was published on 15 October 2026 (DOI: 10.1093/nc/niag071). The correction describes changes to supplementary data, removal of an extraneous supplementary file, and corrections to an equation and table formatting. The correction should be recorded in the source register; its existence does not automatically invalidate the article's results, but it makes it important to use the corrected version and current supplementary materials. [S16]

This paper is not a global verdict on consciousness theories. Its result concerns a specific dataset, a defined visual-perception paradigm, a set of candidate connectivity metrics, and models designed to represent theory-associated dynamics. A model's fit to measured neural patterns is evidence about that model and dataset, not a direct measurement of subjective experience itself.

## 3. Philosophical and cross-cultural perspectives

The empirical question asks which mechanisms predict and explain conscious states and contents. The philosophical question also asks why or how physical or functional processes have a subjective aspect, what counts as an explanation, and whether phenomenal experience can be reduced to or identified with physical processes. These questions interact but should not be conflated.

Physicalist approaches broadly seek an account in physical terms; dualist approaches distinguish mental and physical realities in some way; panpsychist and other views assign consciousness or proto-conscious properties a more fundamental role. Each position contains variants and faces unresolved arguments. Evidence for reliable neural correlates does not, by itself, settle every metaphysical interpretation of those correlations. Nor does the explanatory difficulty alone establish a nonphysical explanation.

Some Indian philosophical traditions identify ultimate reality or the deepest self with consciousness; Buddhist traditions include sophisticated analyses of experience while challenging the idea of a permanent, independent self. These traditions should be described in their own terms rather than treated as one shared doctrine. In particular, “pure consciousness,” “self,” “soul,” and “no-self” do not have identical meanings across traditions. [S05–S10, S17]

A disciplined framework separates:
1. **Phenomenology:** what is experienced or reported.
2. **Function and access:** what information the system can use and how.
3. **Mechanism:** the processes that reliably produce or support the state.
4. **Ontology:** what consciousness ultimately is and what exists independently of a report.
5. **Interpretation and value:** the personal, cultural, ethical, or spiritual meaning assigned to an experience.

Evidence at one level can inform another, but it cannot automatically substitute for it.

## 4. Relationships within the foundational framework

- **Consciousness–body:** Human conscious states are systematically related to brain and bodily conditions. This supports investigation of mechanisms; it does not mean every aspect of consciousness has already been explained.
- **Consciousness–mind:** Conscious experience is one dimension of mental life, not a synonym for all cognition. Some processing occurs without clear conscious access.
- **Consciousness–soul:** Whether consciousness requires a persisting substantial subject, and whether “soul” names such a subject, remain philosophical and religious questions rather than conclusions established by neural correlates.
- **Consciousness–spirit:** A spiritual experience can be studied as a reported experience and as a phenomenon embedded in practices and traditions. Whether its interpretation corresponds to an external or transcendent reality is a further question.
- **Consciousness–Earth:** Ecological interdependence and Earth-system integration do not establish a unified planetary subject. A claim that Earth, the biosphere, or Earth's core has experience would require a clear definition of the proposed subject, a plausible account of relevant organization or mechanism, and evidence that distinguishes the claim from metaphor.
- **Consciousness–Transformative Intelligence:** Reflection and metacognition can support the project's five-phase cycle—Divergence, Insurgence, Allegiance, Convergence, Ascendence—as a normative or operational framework. The cycle is a project model, not an empirically established sequence of conscious states.

These relationships are framework-level organizing proposals. They should not be misrepresented as experimental findings.

## 5. Uncertainty, limits, and prohibited inferences

No universally accepted solution to consciousness follows from the sources reviewed here. The Cogitate study provides an unusually explicit comparison of predictions, but it does not resolve every version of GNWT, IIT, HOT, or the hard problem. The 2026 critiques raise issues that merit examination; they do not, simply by being published, establish that the collaboration was invalid or that alternative physical mechanisms are responsible for experience.

The following claims are **not established by this report**:

- That any current AI system is phenomenally conscious or has human-equivalent experience.
- That memory, language, self-description, attention, information integration, or workspace architecture alone proves consciousness.
- That consciousness has been universally measured by a single numerical index or detector.
- That a neural correlate is automatically a sufficient cause or a complete explanation.
- That consciousness is a fundamental property of the universe, or that a particular metaphysical account has been scientifically proven.
- That Earth, the biosphere, or Earth's core is a unified conscious subject.
- That proposed quantum, magnetic, or piezoelectric mechanisms explain consciousness merely because they are physically conceivable.

The right stance is disciplined uncertainty: preserve what the evidence supports, specify what it cannot decide, and make further claims testable where possible.

## 7. Recommended next research artifact: a theory–evidence matrix

The next step should be a transparent matrix, prepared before any new analysis, with one row per theory-specific prediction and the following fields:

| Field | Required content |
|---|---|
| Theory and version | Precisely identify the version being tested |
| Core commitment | The claim the theory treats as central |
| Operational prediction | Observable result that follows from the commitment |
| Auxiliary assumptions (“imports”) | Biological or methodological assumptions required to connect theory to measurement |
| Competing predictions | What credible alternatives predict under the same conditions |
| Preregistered measure | Exact measure, preprocessing, exclusions, and analysis |
| Pass / fail / ambiguous criteria | Criteria specified before observing the result |
| Result and uncertainty | Effect size, uncertainty, robustness, and deviations |
| Scope of inference | What the result supports and what it cannot establish |
| Replication status | Independent, direct, conceptual, or not yet replicated |

The matrix should distinguish (a) failure of an operational prediction, (b) failure of an auxiliary assumption, and (c) a challenge to a core theoretical commitment. These categories must not be collapsed, and an auxiliary assumption must not become an unrestricted escape hatch after results are known. Equally, critics should not treat failure of one implementation as automatic refutation of every version of a theory. Predefined criteria, transparent data and code, explicit uncertainty, and independent replication would make the next comparison more informative.

## 8. Conclusions

Consciousness remains empirically investigable and deeply contested. The 2025 Cogitate collaboration was a methodological landmark because it preregistered competing predictions and tested them across multiple laboratories and measurement methods. Its mixed findings challenge important predictions of both GNWT and IIT without settling every version of either theory. The 2026 critique by Stewart raises a substantive question about whether operational predictions adequately test central theoretical commitments, but it remains a critique that must be assessed against the primary protocol and evidence. The 2026 connectivity analysis provides a scoped, data-driven comparison of measures and models, and its correction should be incorporated into any replication or follow-up.

The strongest conclusion is not that consciousness has been solved, that all theories are equivalent, or that a new physical or spiritual mechanism has been demonstrated. It is that future research should make the relation between theoretical commitments, operational measures, and permitted conclusions explicit before data collection. Functional simulation and evidence of subjective experience remain distinct claims. A universal consciousness detector, a proven AI consciousness claim, and an Earth-scale conscious subject are not established by the material reviewed here. [S01–S16]
