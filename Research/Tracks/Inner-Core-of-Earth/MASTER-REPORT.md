## Abstract

The Earth’s inner core is a solid, ~1,221.5 km-radius body of iron–nickel alloy at the centre of the planet, under pressures rising to ~364 GPa and temperatures plausibly between ~5,000 and ~6,200 K at the inner-core boundary (ICB). It was discovered seismologically by Inge Lehmann in 1936, inferred to be solid by Birch in 1940, and directly evidenced as solid by the PKJKP shear-wave phase in 2005. Its first-order structure is well characterised: it is seismically anisotropic (P waves travel faster parallel to the rotation axis), it shows a hemispherical dichotomy in velocity and attenuation, and it is separated from the liquid outer core by a first-order boundary with a density jump of ~0.5–0.9 g/cm³. Its growth releases latent heat and light elements that help power the geodynamo.

Beyond these Established claims, the inner core is deeply contested. Its **rotation** relative to the mantle is the subject of two explicit, mutually incompatible peer-reviewed positions in 2026: one claiming that a “consensus” has emerged that differential rotation cannot explain the seismic data and that surface topographic change alone suffices, and another finding a coherent rotation pattern of ~0.10°/yr before ~2005 and near-zero since ~2010. Its **viscosity** is disputed by a factor of ~10³–10⁵: dislocation-creep calculations give 1 × 10¹⁴–2 × 10¹⁶ Pa·s, while homologous-temperature extrapolation of deformation experiments gives ≥~10¹⁹ Pa·s. Its **iron phase** is composition-dependent: hcp is stable for pure Fe, but ~1.4 at.% hydrogen is sufficient to favour bcc, with bcc dominant above ~2.78 at.% H. Its **composition** is degenerate under elasticity alone, though anisotropy constraints point toward Fe–Ni–S, with nickel critical. Its **innermost inner core** may be a chemical stratification — “onion-like layering” induced by Si–C alloying — rather than a sharp structural boundary. Its **age** is loosely bounded at ~0.5–1.5 Gyr, with some models extending beyond 2 Gyr, and its early growth may have included a rapid supercooled phase. The **“new core paradox”** — a long-lived geodynamo against high core thermal conductivity — has a quantified proposed pathway: a delayed Archean transition to mobile-lid plate tectonics enables a dynamo even for conductivity >100 W/m/K. Every claim below is labelled **Established**, **Plausible**, **Contested**, **Philosophical/Spiritual**, **Hypothetical**, or **Indeterminate**. The gate is **INDETERMINATE** because no independent reviewer has examined this track.

## 4. Evidence and findings

### 4.1 Discovery, solidity, and first-order properties

**Established.** The inner core was discovered by **Inge Lehmann in 1936** through a triplication of core-refracted P waves. **Birch (1940)** proposed that the discontinuity corresponded to freezing under central pressure, and **Bullen (1950–51)** predicted the PKJKP shear phase. Direct seismic evidence for solidity came in **2005**, when **Cao, Romanowicz, and Takeuchi** detected PKJKP using stacked broadband records from the Gräfenberg array.

**Established (PREM-based).** The inner-core radius is **~1,221.5 km**; central density is **~13.0885 g/cm³**; maximum pressure is **~364 GPa**; gravity at the ICB is **~4.4002 m/s²**. The inner core is about **0.14% of Earth’s mass**; the whole core is about one-third. The ICB density jump is commonly reported in the range **~0.5–0.9 g/cm³**.

### 4.2 Seismic structure

**Established.** The inner core is **seismically anisotropic**: PKIKP waves travelling parallel to Earth’s rotation axis arrive earlier than those travelling in the equatorial plane. The anisotropy increases with depth.

**Established.** The inner core shows a **hemispherical dichotomy**: one hemisphere exhibits slower direction-averaged velocity, less attenuation, and greater anisotropy; the other exhibits faster velocity and weaker anisotropy.

**Plausible/Contested.** The uppermost **~30–100 km** may be a distinct **isotropic layer**, laterally variable. The lowermost outer core contains a seismically inferred **F-layer** with a flattened velocity gradient, ~100–200 km thick, possibly stratified.

**Contested.** The **innermost inner core (IMIC)** was proposed at **~300 km** radius (Ishii & Dziewonski 2002) and later at **~650 km** (Pham & Tkalčić 2022–2023). A **chemical-stratification model** — “onion-like layering” induced by Si–C alloying — has been proposed as an alternative to a sharp structural boundary. The radius debate may be partly **ill-posed** if the transition is gradual.

### 4.3 Composition

**Established.** The inner core is dominantly **Fe–Ni** with a **~5% density deficit** relative to pure iron at inner-core conditions; the outer core has a deficit of ~8–10%. This requires light elements.

**Plausible.** The leading light-element candidates are **S, Si, O, C, and H**. A 2025 *GRL* study finds that **iron–sulfur fits better than pure iron**, that **no light-element alloy matches the observed anisotropy better than iron–nickel alloy**, and that **nickel is a critical component**. The inner core **“likely consists of iron, nickel, and a small amount of sulfur.”**

**Indeterminate.** A 2026 *JGR Solid Earth* study finds that **millions of distinct hcp-Fe-light-element combinations** satisfy PREM seismic constraints, and that **elasticity alone imposes a much weaker constraint than previously assumed**. Exact abundances remain **unknown**. Anisotropy adds one constraint (pointing to Fe–Ni–S), but it depends on deformation assumptions.

### 4.4 Iron phase and melting

**Contested.** The stable phase of iron at inner-core conditions is **composition-dependent**. A 2026 molecular-dynamics benchmark finds that **hcp Fe is thermodynamically stable with the highest melting temperature**, and that **bcc Fe is metastable for pure Fe**. However, **hydrogen changes the answer**: at 330 GPa and 6,000 K, **~1.4 at.% H** is sufficient to overcome the hcp–bcc free-energy difference and **favour bcc**; **bcc dominates above 2.78 at.% H**. At geochemically plausible H contents of 0–11.3 at.%, **hcp, bcc, or mixed phases** can be stable. **Depth-dependent phase transitions** across 330–360 GPa can arise from H content and temperature variation.

**Contested.** Estimates of the melting temperature of pure iron at the ICB pressure range from ~4,900 K to ~6,200 K. The uncertainty is a major source of uncertainty in composition and thermal history.

### 4.5 Rheology and viscosity

**Contested.** Inner-core viscosity estimates span many orders of magnitude. Dislocation-creep calculations for hcp Fe alloys give **1 × 10¹⁴–2 × 10¹⁶ Pa·s**. Homologous-temperature extrapolation of deformation experiments on hcp-Fe gives **≥~10¹⁹ Pa·s**. The disagreement is **methodological, not empirical**: the approaches probe different deformation regimes and extrapolations. The **translation model** requires the high end; the **surface-change / viscous deformation** model is consistent with the low end.

### 4.6 Rotation vs. surface change

This is the **paradigm-level** dispute of the track.

**Position A (rotation).** Wu, Song, and Yang (2026, *GRL*) find that the inner core **“successively rotated faster than the mantle by about 0.10°/yr”** before ~2005, and **“has moved very little since about 2010.”** They interpret this as a **coherent rotation pattern** consistent with a multidecadal oscillation.

**Position B (surface change alone).** Zhang and Wen (2026, *JGR Solid Earth*) state that **“a consensus has emerged that the hypothesis of inner core differential rotation cannot provide a consistent explanation for the seismic data.”** They argue that **“the data contradictions to the hypothesis of inner core differential rotation are extensive”** and that **“temporal changes of the inner core surface provide a simple and consistent explanation for the seismic data without any inner core differential rotation.”** They map **episodic change, quiescence, and recovery** beneath eastern South America (2003–2024) and propose **localized viscous deformation** and **localized alternate solidification/melting** as mechanisms.

**x12 assessment.** The two positions are **peer-reviewed, 2026, and mutually incompatible**. They disagree on **the correct variable** (rotation vs. surface change), not merely on the rate. The honest label is **Contested — paradigm-level**. The **Indeterminate** element is **which variable — rotation, surface change, or both — is necessary and sufficient** to explain the seismic data.

### 4.7 The core paradox

**Contested.** The **“new core paradox”** is the difficulty of sustaining a geodynamo for ~3.4–4.3 Gyr when high core thermal conductivity makes thermal convection before inner-core nucleation inefficient.

**Plausible pathway.** Bonnet Gibet et al. (2026, arXiv, submitted to *jSEDI*) couple mantle and core thermal evolution in a 1D model with a **parametrized transition from stagnant-lid to mobile-lid convection**. Their Bayesian inversion favours a **transition during the Archean**, which **“promotes core cooling and enables a geodynamo throughout Earth’s history, even for core thermal conductivities in excess of 100 W/m/K.”** A companion EGU 2026 abstract reports that a **late and rapid transition** allows conductivity **up to 110 W/m/K**.

**x12 assessment.** The paradox is **not resolved**, but it now has a **quantified, physically motivated solution space**. The key **Indeterminate** parameter is the **onset time and duration** of the stagnant-to-mobile-lid transition.

### 4.8 Age and growth

**Plausible.** The inner core is **young** relative to Earth. Estimates cluster around **~0.5–1.5 Gyr**, with some arguments extending to **~2 Gyr** or older. A 2025 *Nature Reviews Earth & Environment* paper argues that **supercooling of at least 450 K** is needed to nucleate the inner core, implying **two growth regimes**: an initial **rapid phase** (cm/yr, potentially freezing in **<100 years**) followed by slower **equilibrium growth** (mm/yr). The rapid phase could have produced a region at least as large as the **innermost inner core (250–700 km radius)**.

**Indeterminate.** The precise age is not known to better than a factor of about three. Paleointensity data have been argued to be insufficient to constrain the timing of inner-core formation.

## 10. Limitations and counterevidence

### 10.1 Method limitations

- **No direct sampling.** The inner core has never been sampled. Composition is inferred from seismic velocities and densities, high-pressure experiments, cosmochemistry, and partitioning models.
- **Non-uniqueness.** Different combinations of light elements, temperature, and phase can fit the same seismic observations. A 2026 study finds **millions of distinct compositions** satisfy PREM.
- **Resolution limits.** Thin layers (F-layer, uppermost isotropic layer) and sharp boundaries (IMIC) are near or below seismic resolution.
- **Data scarcity.** Deep earthquakes and antipodal paths are required for PKJKP and IMIC studies; the dataset is small and unevenly distributed.
- **Extrapolation.** Laboratory experiments at 330–364 GPa and 5,000–6,200 K are at the edge of current capability; theoretical calculations depend on exchange-correlation functionals and phase-space sampling.

### 10.2 Counterevidence and unresolved tensions

- The **rotation signal** is small and contested. Zhang & Wen (2026) state that data contradictions to differential rotation are extensive; Wu et al. (2026) find a coherent pattern. The positions are peer-reviewed and incompatible in framing.
- The **viscosity disagreement** is methodological, not empirical. Both 10¹⁴–10¹⁶ and ≥10¹⁹ Pa·s are extrapolations from limited data.
- The **IMIC** is not universally accepted; the radius debate may be ill-posed if the transition is chemical and gradual.
- The **translation model** is challenged on structural grounds (sharp boundaries, no regional correlation) and by viscosity uncertainty.
- The **core paradox** is not resolved; proposed models require specific parameters or tectonic histories.
- The **light-element budget** is not unique; S, Si, O, and C each have support, but no single budget satisfies all constraints.
