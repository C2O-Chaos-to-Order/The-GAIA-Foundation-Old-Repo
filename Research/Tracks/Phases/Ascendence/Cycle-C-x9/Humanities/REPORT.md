- **ASC-HUM-01** [P] P89 finds that Liverpool English is characterised by low and rising contours: the most common phrase-final contour in Liverpool is L* L-H% (Knowles' 'rise'; 54% of tokens vs 7% in Manchester), with rise-plateau-slump used more by men (145 tokens, 34%) than women (48, 13%); Manchester uses mainly falls (H* L-L%), and very high final rises (possible HRT) were only six of 495 declaratives, all in Manchester. P89 argues the Liverpool rise came from new-dialect formation after rapid nineteenth-century growth (Lawton: 1349% in 1773-1871) with Irish input, a case where a minority variant was favoured. *Operationalization:* HUM Ascendence = rising intonation in Liverpool English. *Sources:* P89. *Limits:* Read speech only; the historical account is argued, not tested. Slip: the text labels the Liverpool 'rise' L* H-H% in the results summary and in the Fig. 3 caption, although it defines L* H-H% as the 'rise-plateau' and tests L* L-H% as the most common Liverpool contour.
- **ASC-HUM-04** [P] Rising pitch carries social meaning that depends on the listener: in P92's matched-guise study of young women's speech, uptalk was rated more negatively overall (mean 40.8 vs 54.9 neutral and 51.8 creak), ratings largely followed predicted iconic pitch associations, and older and younger listeners differed (uptalk 43.6 vs 38.0). P92 concludes that the availability of iconic associations of pitch depends on social factors. Audit: counts (71 - 11 = 60; 60 x 15 x 8 = 7200), means, Table 4 mean squares, a common residual MS (272.0, 271.6) and the two F p-values reproduce. *Operationalization:* HUM Ascendence = rising intonation (uptalk) and its meaning. *Sources:* SIM-AS-HUM-B/iter-1#V1;SIM-AS-HUM-B/iter-1#H1;SIM-AS-HUM-B/iter-1#H2;SIM-AS-HUM-B/iter-1#H3;SIM-AS-HUM-B/iter-1#H4;P92. *Limits:* Slips: 'p = .0503' listed among significant differences; a 'Section 1.5' cross-reference with no such section; the model formula is garbled in the PMC text. - **ASC-HUM-03** [H] In a 200-speaker copying toy, majority-amplifying copying (c 1.5, a stand-in for Trudgill's majority rule) eliminated a variant starting at 15% (P89's Irish share in 1871) or 30% in every run within about 3-5 generations, and selection b 0.3 for the variant did not rescue it (fixation 0 from 15%, 0.019 from 30%; both pre-registered rescue checks not supported); under neutral copying a 15% variant fixed in 15.4% of runs. In this toy a minority rise such as Liverpool's needs either much stronger selection or a copying rule without majority amplification. *Operationalization:* HUM Ascendence = selection of a minority variant. *Sources:* SIM-AS-HUM-A/iter-1#V1;SIM-AS-HUM-A/iter-1#H1;SIM-AS-HUM-A/iter-1#H2;SIM-AS-HUM-A/iter-2#V1;SIM-AS-HUM-A/iter-2#H1;SIM-AS-HUM-A/iter-2#H2;SIM-AS-HUM-A/iter-3#V1;SIM-AS-HUM-A/iter-3#H1. *Limits:* Toy; N, c, b and the start share are the agent's choices; P89's account involves many features, social networks and Irish input over decades.
- **ASC-HUM-05** [H] In the copying toy, a 15% variant is lost in every run under weaker conformity (c 1.2), and even strong selection (b 1.0) against c 1.5 fixes it in only 5.9% of runs (pre-registered rescue not supported; from 30% it fixes in every run). Without majority amplification, modest selection suffices: b 0.1 fixes it in 99.8% (N 200) and b 0.05 in 100% (N 1000), and c 1.2 with b 0.5 in 86.7%. A minority rise in the toy hinges on the copying rule more than on the strength of selection. *Operationalization:* HUM Ascendence = selection of a minority variant. *Sources:* SIM-AS-HUM-B/iter-2#V1;SIM-AS-HUM-B/iter-2#H1;SIM-AS-HUM-B/iter-3#V1;SIM-AS-HUM-B/iter-3#H1;SIM-AS-HUM-B/iter-4#V1;SIM-AS-HUM-B/iter-4#H1;SIM-AS-HUM-B/iter-5#V1;SIM-AS-HUM-B/iter-5#H1;SIM-AS-HUM-B/iter-6#V1;SIM-AS-HUM-B/iter-6#H1. *Limits:* Toy; N, c, b and start shares are the agent's choices.
