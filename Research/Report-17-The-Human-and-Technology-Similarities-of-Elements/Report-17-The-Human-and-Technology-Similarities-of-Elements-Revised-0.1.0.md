## 1. Definition and central claim

The **human–technology elemental similarity** proposed here is a comparison among the composition, organization, and functions of biological and engineered systems. It is not a claim that humans and devices have identical material composition, nor that all devices are primarily silicon. The strongest defensible version is that both biological and engineered systems are organized physical systems whose materials and behaviors are constrained by the same chemistry and physics.

This report distinguishes three dimensions that must not be conflated:

1. **Compositional comparison:** which elements and materials are present, and in what proportions.
2. **Structural comparison:** how components are arranged, connected, layered, regulated, and coupled.
3. **Functional comparison:** what operations the system performs—metabolism, sensing, signalling, computation, energy storage, repair, or manufacture.

The report's central GAIA proposal is that the periodic table provides a common reference framework for comparing material possibilities, while biology and technology embody different material selections and organizational solutions. This is a conceptual framework built on chemistry, not a new physical law.

## 2. Compositional contrast: accurate, but not a simple binary

### 2.1 Human-body composition

A widely used reference estimate for a 70 kg adult gives approximately 43 kg oxygen, 16 kg carbon, 7 kg hydrogen, and 1.8 kg nitrogen—about 96.6% of total body mass. The remaining mass includes calcium, phosphorus, sulfur, potassium, sodium, chlorine, magnesium, iron, zinc, copper, iodine, and other elements. Actual composition varies with body size, age, body-water fraction, tissue distribution, and health.

The supplied draft's example of about 46 kg oxygen and 13 kg carbon is not the same as the standard reference table checked for this revision. Use a clearly identified reference model rather than presenting one set of figures as universal.

Source: CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics, “Chemical Composition of the Human Body” (standard 70 kg reference man table), accessible copy hosted by Harvard BioNumbers: https://kirschner.med.harvard.edu/files/bionumbers/CRC%20Handbook-Chemical%20Composition%20of%20the%20Human%20Body.pdf.

### 2.2 Smartphone and technology composition

A smartphone is not adequately described as simply “silicon-based and metal-rich.” It is a composite system containing a mix of glass and ceramics, polymers, battery materials, semiconductor materials, copper and other conductors, steel or aluminum components, adhesives, and numerous metals in small quantities. Composition depends on device generation, manufacturer, model, which parts are counted, and whether the analysis measures the whole device, circuit boards, camera modules, magnets, or recoverable metals.

A study of 45 mobile phones manufactured between 1998 and 2021 found that copper, nickel, and tin can account for up to 65% of printed-circuit-board mass, with concentrations changing over time. A separate study of older smartphone components found that copper, nickel, tin, zinc, and iron dominated the measured metal content of selected components, while rare-earth elements and platinum-group metals together were only a small fraction of recoverable element mass. Other device analyses show that silicon and aluminum are present in glass and display materials and that rare-earth elements have specific applications in components such as magnets and displays.

Sources:
- Jandric et al., “Material composition trends in printed circuit boards of mobile phones,” Resources, Conservation & Recycling 227 (2026), 108741. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resconrec.2025.108741.
- Tantawi & Hua, “Temporal evolution of metallic element composition and environmental impact in consumer electronic devices: A study of smartphones,” Resources, Conservation & Recycling 175 (2021), 105886. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resconrec.2021.105886.
- “Metallic resources in smartphones,” Resources Policy (2020), 101750. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resourpol.2020.101750.

### 2.3 What “overlap is minimal” can and cannot mean

The claim that elemental overlap between the human body and technology is “minimal” is not established by naming a few dominant elements. Humans and smartphones both contain many of the same elements, including oxygen, carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, silicon, iron, copper, zinc, and others, although their amounts, chemical forms, and functional roles differ. The phrase “minimal overlap” requires a defined metric and a specified device/system boundary.

Possible metrics include:
- **Presence/absence overlap:** number of shared element identities divided by the union of the element sets.
- **Mass-fraction similarity:** a specified distance measure between normalized elemental mass-fraction vectors.
- **Functional overlap:** whether an element's role in each system is comparable, which requires a role taxonomy.
- **Spatial or structural correspondence:** similarity of organization at a specified scale.

These metrics answer different questions and can yield different results. Until a device is specified and a metric chosen, report the contrast qualitatively: human tissue is dominated by water and carbon-rich biomolecules, while engineered devices have material distributions shaped by their components and design requirements.

## 3. Shared periodic grammar: a useful but carefully scoped interpretation

The periodic table organizes elements by atomic number and recurring chemical properties. Valence, oxidation states, electronegativity, bonding, conductivity, and other properties help constrain what materials can do. Biological evolution and engineering both operate within these constraints, but in different ways.

Biological systems use elements in chemically specific roles: iron in heme proteins, zinc in many enzymes and regulatory proteins, copper in electron-transfer proteins, phosphorus in nucleic acids and energy-transfer compounds, and calcium in mineralized tissue and signalling. These examples do not mean the body “chooses” elements consciously; evolutionary selection and biochemical constraints shape the resulting systems.

Engineered systems exploit properties through deliberate materials selection and design: silicon and other semiconductors in electronics, copper in electrical conductors, lithium-containing compounds in many rechargeable batteries, and neodymium-containing magnets in certain motors and speakers. Gallium-containing semiconductors are used in particular optoelectronic applications. The specific choices vary by device and application.

The shared periodic table is therefore a common **constraint space**, not necessarily an “active agent.” Biology is shaped by evolution, environmental availability, and biochemical organization; engineering is shaped by design goals, manufacturing, cost, safety, and supply chains. The analogy is strongest when these different pathways are kept visible.

## 4. Functional parallel: metabolism and engineering

The human body continuously transforms matter and energy through digestion, absorption, metabolism, biosynthesis, tissue maintenance, and waste elimination. Atoms are incorporated into biomolecules and tissues or released through metabolic processes. Most ordinary metabolism changes chemical bonds and molecular arrangements; it does not transmute one chemical element into another.

Technology transforms raw materials into components and assembled systems through extraction, refining, chemical processing, fabrication, assembly, computation, and energy conversion. These operations also mostly rearrange atoms and chemical bonds. Nuclear transmutation is a distinct process that changes the atomic nucleus and should not be conflated with metabolism, manufacturing, or ordinary chemical transformations.

“Alchemical” can be used here as a **structural metaphor** for turning raw materials into organized capability. It should not imply that metabolism or manufacturing is nuclear transmutation, or that the symbolic meaning of alchemy is empirically proven by these processes. Report 16 distinguishes literal nuclear transmutation from symbolic/philosophical alchemy; Report 17 adopts the same boundary.

A more precise formulation is:
- **Biological transformation:** self-maintaining, regulated chemical and physical processes under evolutionary constraints.
- **Technological transformation:** designed or managed material and information processes under engineering constraints.
- **Shared principle:** organized systems transform matter and energy into functions.
- **Important difference:** these systems differ in origin, regulation, autonomy, maintenance, reproduction, and goals.

## 5. Biomimicry: strong examples of structural analogy

Biomimicry supports the report's distinction between imitating a function or organization and copying an organism's exact elemental composition.

Johns Hopkins engineers reported a material called Liquid Infused Porous Piezoelectric Scaffold (LIPPS), inspired by the way bone adapts to stress. Under repeated loading, the scaffold's piezoelectric material generates charges that attract ions from an electrolyte; minerals accumulate in loaded regions and can reinforce the material. The design imitates an adaptive organizational principle rather than reproducing the full composition or biology of bone.

Source: Jonathan Deutschman, “No Bones About It: New Material Grows Stronger and More Resilient Under Stress,” Johns Hopkins Whiting School of Engineering, February 24, 2025: https://engineering.jhu.edu/news/no-bones-about-it-new-material-grows-stronger-and-more-resilient-under-stress/. The research paper is reported as published in Science Advances.

This is a useful example of **functional/structural analogy**. It does not demonstrate that human tissue and technology are elementally similar in general, and it does not establish that every biomimetic design uses minerals or electrolyte in the same way as bone. The supplied KAIST bone-like composite claim remains pending exact primary-source recovery in this pass and should not be treated as verified here.

## 6. Chemical representation and machine navigation

The supplied “Chemical Imitation Game” reference is verifiable as a 2026 perspective by Stefano Moro in the Journal of Chemical Information and Modeling. It argues that chemistry and language have parallel representational histories: practitioners' tacit knowledge becomes formal symbols, representations, and computationally navigable spaces. It proposes an evaluative “Chemical Imitation Game” for assessing whether molecular AI outputs are chemically meaningful, rather than merely syntactically valid.

This is relevant to the report's analogy between a periodic table as a map of material possibilities and biological/technological systems as different organized outcomes. It does not claim that biology and technology are mathematically isomorphic, that the periodic table is a consciousness map, or that a single shared geometry of human and technological systems has been empirically demonstrated.

Source: Stefano Moro, “The Chemical Imitation Game: Navigating Spaces of Meaning in Language and Chemistry,” Journal of Chemical Information and Modeling 66(17) (2026), 10457–10465. https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jcim.6c01773.

## 7. Perspectives

**Materialist.** The compositional and mechanistic differences matter most. Biological systems are water-rich and built around complex carbon chemistry; engineered devices use materials selected for their intended functions. This view correctly resists treating analogy as identity.

**Structuralist.** The shared constraint framework matters: both systems are physical organizations built from elements whose properties are described by the same chemistry. This is a useful comparison, but “same periodic grammar” remains a conceptual framing rather than a measured similarity score.

**Functionalist.** Both biological and engineered systems transform matter and energy, process signals, and interact with environments. The functions and mechanisms can be compared, provided the differences in autonomy, regulation, maintenance, reproduction, and design are not erased.

**Archetypal.** The proposed one-to-one mapping between the periodic table and a topological table of archetypes remains speculative. It is not supported by the compositional or biomimicry evidence reviewed here. The hypothesis needs precise definitions, preregistered predictions, controls, and independent replication before any stronger claim is justified.

**Alchemical.** “Modern alchemy” can describe the organized transformation of raw materials into capabilities as a metaphor. It must be distinguished from literal nuclear transmutation and from historical claims about alchemical traditions.

The proposed architecture may use “Body,” “Mind,” “Spirit,” “Soul,” and “Transformation” as conceptual categories. The empirical references must remain distinct: atomic composition, biological regulation, neural activity, electromagnetic phenomena, and engineered computation are not interchangeable merely because a framework places them in corresponding rows.

The phrase “Quantum Entanglement tier” must remain metaphorical unless a claim specifies quantum states, observables, formalism, and testable predictions. A relationship between two systems, a shared physical constraint, or a symbolic notion of non-separability does not by itself establish quantum entanglement.

## 9. Operationalization plan

Any future quantitative assessment of human–technology elemental similarity should define:

1. **System boundary:** whole adult body, selected tissue, whole smartphone, printed circuit board, battery, magnet, or another explicitly named system.
2. **Sample identity:** reference body model and device model/year; sample size and handling.
3. **Elemental assay:** method, detection limits, calibration, uncertainty, and whether values are mass fractions, atom fractions, or presence/absence.
4. **Metric:** set overlap, compositional distance, functional-role overlap, or structural correspondence; specify the formula before seeing results.
5. **Comparison baseline:** null or shuffled comparison and sensitivity to different device classes.
6. **Interpretive limit:** a similarity score for composition does not establish similarity of structure, function, intelligence, consciousness, or value.

No such new quantitative comparison was run for this report. The report does not assign a numerical score to “shared grammar” or “functional similarity.”

## 10. Uncertainty and correction register

1. **Body composition:** reference values vary; the standard 70 kg reference table checked here gives 43 kg oxygen, 16 kg carbon, 7 kg hydrogen, and 1.8 kg nitrogen, not the draft's alternative figures.
2. **Device composition:** “technology” is too broad to assign one elemental profile; device, component, and generation matter.
3. **“Minimal overlap”:** not established without a defined element set and metric. Many elements occur in both biological and engineered systems but at different concentrations and in different chemical forms.
4. **“Silicon-based technology”:** too broad as a generalization; silicon is crucial to many semiconductors, but devices also contain polymers, glass, ceramics, copper, iron, aluminum, battery materials, and other components.
5. **Rare-earth elements:** important in selected functions, but generally not the largest contributors by mass to a whole smartphone or its circuit board.
6. **Shared grammar:** the periodic table is a common reference and constraint system, not an agent that both systems consciously navigate.
7. **Metabolism and manufacturing:** both transform matter, but ordinary biochemical and industrial processes do not generally transmute elements.
8. **Biomimicry:** the Johns Hopkins LIPPS example supports a specific organizational analogy, not a universal claim about human–technology similarity.
9. **KAIST source:** exact primary-source record for the supplied description remains pending.
10. **Chemical Imitation Game:** the verified article is a perspective on molecular representation and molecular AI, not empirical proof of human–technology elemental isomorphism.
11. **Archetypal isomorphism:** speculative and not validated by the sources reviewed.
12. **Quantum entanglement:** symbolic non-separability is not a demonstration of physical entanglement.

## 12. Conclusions

The most defensible human–technology comparison is not that the two systems share few or many elements in some universal sense. It is that they have different, context-dependent material compositions while being governed by the same chemistry and physics. Both biological and engineered systems organize matter into structures and functions, but they differ in how they originate, regulate themselves, maintain organization, reproduce, and pursue outcomes.

The periodic table can serve as the shared **map of elemental possibilities**. Biology and technology realize different paths through that space. Biomimicry offers concrete cases in which engineered systems reproduce selected organizational or adaptive principles of living systems without copying their complete composition. The Chemical Imitation Game offers a related example of how chemical representations can become computationally navigable; it does not establish a universal human–technology isomorphism.

For GAIA, the careful formulation is: **composition is measurable; structure and function are comparable when operationalized; the shared periodic grammar is a useful conceptual model; archetypal correspondences remain speculative; and “modern alchemy” is metaphorical unless it explicitly refers to nuclear transmutation.**

The map is useful because it organizes what can be compared. It must not be mistaken for the systems it represents.

## Additional source-audit checkpoint — 2026-10-23

Jandric et al., “Material composition trends in printed circuit boards of mobile phones,” *Resources, Conservation & Recycling* 227 (2026), article 108741, DOI https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resconrec.2025.108741, analyzes 45 phones manufactured between 1998 and 2021. It reports that copper, nickel, and tin can constitute up to 65 mass percent of smartphone printed-circuit boards in the sampled set, while each other analyzed element contributed less than one percent of the median PCB mass. This finding is specifically about PCBs, not the entire phone, battery, screen, or every device model.

A 2021 smartphone-component study, “Temporal evolution of metallic element composition and environmental impact in consumer electronic devices,” DOI https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resconrec.2021.105886, found that the highest-concentration metals in three analyzed components were Cu, Ni, Sn, Zn, and Fe, while rare-earth elements and platinum-group metals together accounted for a small fraction of total recoverable element mass in that sample. Functional importance must not be conflated with mass abundance.

**Correction boundary:** comparisons between human and technology elemental composition require matched boundaries, denominators, sampling methods, and metrics. “Shared periodic grammar” is a chemical constraint/analogy, not evidence that humans and devices are compositionally identical or quantum-entangled. The device-specific study cannot support a universal statement about all technology.
